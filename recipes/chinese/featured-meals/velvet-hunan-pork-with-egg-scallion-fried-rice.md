# Velvet Hunan Pork with Egg-Scallion Fried Rice

> Melt-in-your-mouth velveted pork butt tossed with blistered green and red jalapeños, sharp Thai chile, garlic, ginger, chili crisp, and a concentrated savory Hunan-style glaze, served with clean egg-scallion fried rice made from chicken-broth rice. The pork stays silky, the peppers stay loud, and the sauce clings instead of pooling.

## Before Cooking Day / Advance Prep

- **Rice first** — Cook **1 cup basmati rice in 1 3/4 cups chicken broth**. As soon as it finishes, spread it in a thin layer on a sheet pan, platter, or wide baking dish so steam can escape. Cool, then refrigerate uncovered or very loosely covered until the grains are cold and surface-dry.
- **Pork stays cold** — Keep the sliced pork refrigerated until **35 to 45 minutes before its cooking stage**. Do not leave thin raw slices on the counter for an extended tempering period.
- **Velvet timing** — Apply the measured velvet **30 to 40 minutes before cooking the pork**. Do not exceed the baking-soda amount and do not hold the velveted pork for hours.
- **Current live-test note** — The first test batch had already been lightly fork-tenderized before this card was written. Do not repeat mechanical tenderizing. Future tests should determine whether the chemical/water velvet alone gives the preferred texture.
- **One-skillet rule** — Fried rice cooks first. Hunan pork cooks last. Once the pork enters its final toss, dinner is going to the table.

## Do Not Forget

- **Rice leaves the cooker immediately.** Spread it thin; trapped steam is the enemy of fried rice.
- **Pork stays refrigerated** until the 30- to 40-minute velvet window.
- **Use exactly 1/2 tsp baking soda per 1 lb pork.** More is not better.
- **Do not rinse the pork after velveting.** The absorbed water and starch coat are the tenderness system.
- **Pork is 1/8-inch thick across the grain.** Thick or with-grain slices will not give the same melt-in-your-mouth result.
- **Jalapeños are not rings.** Stem, halve lengthwise, then cut the halves on a 45° bias into 1-inch pieces so broad surfaces can blister.
- **Do not add oil or water when blistering peppers.** They need dry direct contact first.
- **Cook the pork in two batches if a single layer is not possible.** Crowding creates steam and extends cooking time.
- **The pork precook is intentionally incomplete.** Remove when the slices are mostly opaque but still look slightly underdone in the centers.
- **Do not brown the pork hard.** Silky tenderness matters more than crust in this recipe.
- **The final sauce is a glaze, not gravy.** There should be no pool of brown liquid in the skillet.
- **The last minute is the dish.** Once pork, peppers, aromatics, and sauce reunite, stay at the skillet and pull it as soon as the meat is 145°F and glossy.
- **Ancho and guajillo are not used in this build.** Their deep fruity/smoky character pulls this specific dish away from the fresh-chile Hunan direction.

## Full Cooking Timeline and Instructions

1. **Cook and dry the rice first.** Rinse **1 cup basmati rice** until the water runs mostly clear. Cook it with **1 3/4 cups chicken broth**. The moment the rice is done, get it out of the cooker; spread it in a thin, loose layer on a sheet pan, platter, or wide baking dish. Do not compress it. Let visible steam escape, then refrigerate until cold and surface-dry. You are looking for grains that separate when lifted with the fingers rather than clumping into a soft mass.

2. **Slice and velvet the pork 30 to 40 minutes before its cook.** The pork should be **1/8-inch thick and cut across the grain**. In its bowl, add **2 Tbsp cold water, 2 tsp Shaoxing wine or chicken broth, 2 tsp oyster sauce, 1 tsp soy sauce, and 1/2 tsp baking soda**. Massage firmly for **60 to 90 seconds**, until the pork has absorbed most of the visible liquid and feels tacky rather than wet. Add **2 tsp cornstarch** and massage again until every slice is lightly coated. Add **2 tsp neutral oil** last and fold through. Cover and refrigerate **30 to 40 minutes**. Do not leave it at room temperature.

3. **Cut every vegetable before the skillet heats.** Stem **4 green jalapeños and 2 red jalapeños**. Halve each lengthwise. For the house heat level, retain the ribs and seeds. Lay each half cut-side down and cut on a **45° bias into 1-inch pieces**. Stem **1 Thai chile** and slice it into **1/8-inch rounds**. Smash and peel **6 garlic cloves**, then rough-chop them into **1/8- to 1/4-inch pieces**. Finely mince **1 Tbsp fresh ginger**. For the rice, cut **1/4 cup onion into 1/4-inch dice**, finely mince **3 additional garlic cloves**, and slice **3 green onions 1/8-inch thick**, separating the white/light-green pieces from the dark-green tops.

4. **Mix both seasoning bowls before heat.** For Bowl R4, stir together **1 Tbsp soy sauce, 1 tsp oyster sauce, 1 Tbsp chicken broth, and 1/4 tsp MSG**. For Bowl P4, stir together **1 Tbsp soy sauce, 2 tsp oyster sauce, 1 tsp dark soy sauce or regular soy sauce, 1 Tbsp chili crisp, 2 tsp chili garlic sauce, 1 tsp rice vinegar, 1/2 tsp sugar, 1/4 tsp MSG, and 2 Tbsp chicken broth**. Keep them beside the skillet with every other bowl in order.

5. **Cook the fried rice first at 325°F.** Preheat the electric skillet to **325°F**. Add **1 tsp of the 2 Tbsp neutral oil**, then pour in **2 beaten eggs**. Stir and fold only until softly set, about **30 to 45 seconds**; transfer the eggs back to their bowl. Add **2 tsp more oil**, then add **1/4 cup diced onion and the white/light-green portions of 3 green onions**. Stir-fry **60 to 90 seconds** until aromatic and just beginning to soften. Add **3 minced garlic cloves** and cook **15 to 20 seconds**—do not brown them dark.

6. **Fry the cold rice until the grains wake back up.** Add the **about 3 cups cold chicken-broth rice** to the skillet. Add the remaining neutral oil as needed, using **no more than the full 2 Tbsp total for the rice**. Break up clumps with the flat edge of the spatula, then spread the rice across the skillet and leave it alone for **45 seconds**. Toss, spread again, and cook another **45 to 60 seconds**. When the grains feel dry, hot, and separate, pour Bowl R4 around the rice rather than into one wet spot. Toss constantly for **45 seconds**. Return the soft egg pieces, add the **dark-green scallion tops and 1 tsp sesame oil**, toss **20 seconds**, taste, and remove the rice to a warm serving bowl. Cover only loosely so steam does not turn it soft.

7. **Wipe the skillet and blister the jalapeños dry.** Wipe out loose rice or egg bits. Return the electric skillet to **325°F**. Add the **4 green and 2 red jalapeños with no oil and no water**. Spread them into one layer with as much cut surface touching the skillet as possible. Leave them undisturbed **45 to 60 seconds**, then toss and press again. Continue **3 to 5 minutes total**, until the peppers soften slightly, smell intensely fresh and hot, and carry dark blistered patches without collapsing. Transfer them to Bowl P2.

8. **Velvet-cook the pork without taking it all the way.** Add **1 Tbsp neutral oil** to the 325°F skillet. If the skillet is extremely hot or the coating begins darkening immediately, reduce to **300°F**. Add only enough pork for a true single layer. Let the first surface set for **25 to 30 seconds**, then turn and separate the slices. Stir only until the slices are **mostly opaque but still slightly underdone at the centers**, about **60 to 90 seconds total depending on thickness**. Transfer immediately to a clean holding bowl. Repeat with the second batch, adding the reserved **1 Tbsp oil only if the skillet is dry**. Do not chase browning; the texture target is velvet.

9. **Build the Hunan aromatic base.** With the pork out, set the skillet to **300°F**. Leave only a thin film of oil in the pan. Add **6 rough-chopped garlic cloves, 1 Tbsp minced ginger, and the sliced Thai chile**. Stir continuously **15 to 20 seconds**, just until the garlic smells sweet and the Thai chile blooms. Do not let the garlic turn dark brown.

10. **Finish in one fast, uninterrupted toss.** Return the blistered jalapeños and all precooked pork to the skillet. Toss **15 seconds** to distribute the aromatics. Stir Bowl P4 once more, then pour the measured Hunan sauce around the hot skillet. Toss continuously **45 to 60 seconds**. The sauce should boil almost immediately, reduce, and turn glossy around the meat and peppers. Check the thickest representative pork slices; once they reach **145°F**, the meat feels flexible rather than firm, and there is no watery sauce pooling underneath, **turn the skillet off and get the pork out**. Do not leave it sitting on the hot electric surface.

11. **Serve immediately.** Spoon the egg-scallion fried rice beside or under the Velvet Hunan Pork. Give the plate enough pork glaze and blistered pepper pieces that every bite of rice can meet the main dish, but do not drown the rice. Put extra chili crisp on the table rather than automatically adding more to the skillet. The pork should arrive hot, glossy, and almost melt when chewed; the peppers should still have shape and a smoky-fresh bite.

## Final Taste Target

The first impression should be **fresh chile, garlic, pork, and savory heat**, not sweetness and not brown sauce. The pork should be exceptionally soft and juicy with no chewiness at the edges. Green jalapeño brings grassy heat, red jalapeño brings ripe sweetness, Thai chile gives a clean high note, and chili crisp/chili garlic sauce add roasted depth. Rice vinegar should register as lift rather than obvious sourness. MSG, oyster sauce, soy, broth, and pork juices should create a deep umami finish without the funky fermented character Amy and Don dislike. The fried rice stays deliberately cleaner than the pork: savory, eggy, scallion-forward, separated, and dry enough to absorb the pork glaze.

## Chef's Notes

- The electric skillet is an advantage here if we respect its heat. Its wide surface gives the pork and peppers contact; its danger is holding heat after the food is done. **Remove finished pork from the skillet instead of merely turning the dial down.**
- If the pork starts releasing liquid during the precook, there is too much meat in the skillet at once. Pull half out immediately and finish in batches.
- If the sauce is still loose after 60 seconds, remove the pork and peppers first, reduce the sauce alone for 15 to 30 seconds, then toss everything back through once. Do not cook the pork longer just to reduce liquid.
- If the finished dish needs more heat, add chili crisp at the table. If it needs more brightness, use a few drops of rice vinegar in the serving platter rather than adding a large acidic splash to the pan.
- For a larger pork batch, scale the velvet, pepper, aromatic, and sauce components **proportionally by pork weight**, but continue cooking the meat in one-pound-or-smaller skillet loads so texture does not deteriorate.

## Source / Research Notes

### Traditional / Strong Source Pattern

Research for this build centered on **湖南小炒肉 / 辣椒炒肉 (Hunan xiao chao rou / pork fried with chiles)** rather than the American restaurant category of thick-sauced “Hunan pork.” Across Hunan-oriented Chinese recipe sources, the recurring architecture is:

- fresh green chile is a principal ingredient, not garnish;
- pork and peppers may approach a roughly 1:1 weight relationship in chile-forward versions;
- peppers are often dry-fried or pressed separately until softened and blistered before meeting the pork;
- pork and peppers are commonly cooked separately and reunited late so neither is overcooked;
- garlic, soy, douchi, salt, and MSG/chicken seasoning are common savory anchors;
- the finished dish is concentrated and oily/glossy rather than thickened into a large volume of brown gravy;
- shoulder/front-leg pork and pork belly both appear in strong versions, often with a mix of lean and fatty meat.

Sources reviewed included multiple Hunan-focused **Xiachufang** preparations, including restaurant-style and Changsha/Hunan home-cook versions, plus English-language Chinese-cooking references used to compare pepper treatment and velvet technique.

### Amy & Don Texture Requirement

Strict dry-fried versions can produce a firmer, drier pork bite than Amy and Don prefer. Their target is explicitly **velvet, soft, melt-in-the-mouth meat**. This recipe therefore keeps the Hunan fresh-chile architecture but uses a full water/baking-soda/cornstarch/oil velvet and an intentionally incomplete pork precook before the final toss.

This is not a compromise to “home cooking.” It is a deliberate Flavorweaver texture calibration: preserve the dish's chile-pork identity while making the meat texture match the house standard.

### Pantry Adaptation

- **Douchi / fermented black beans:** unavailable for this test. Depth is rebuilt with chili crisp, chili garlic sauce, oyster sauce, soy, MSG, browned pork juices, garlic, and ginger.
- **White pepper:** unavailable and not replaced with black pepper; it is omitted rather than adding a different dominant note.
- **Fresh Hunan long green chiles / luosijiao:** unavailable. Green and red jalapeños provide the bulk fresh-chile body; Thai chile restores sharper heat.
- **Dried ancho and guajillo:** intentionally excluded. Both are valuable pantry chiles, but their fruit/smoke profile pulls this dish away from the clean fresh-chile identity we want tonight.
- **Chicken-broth rice:** deliberate house move. The fried rice is seasoned more lightly because the rice itself begins savory.
