# Don's Garlic-Citrus Carnitas Tacos

> Garlic-citrus pork butt cooked slowly until succulent in its own rendered fat, broken into substantial pieces, hard-crisped with concentrated pork jus, and served in warm white-corn tortillas with a fiery charred chile-lime salsa finished with cilantro, Tajín, smoked paprika, warm spices, and bright acid. The live-tested meal became Flavorweaver Canon after Don called it the best taco he had eaten in a long time.

## Before Cooking Day / Advance Prep

- **24 hours before cooking** — Coat the pork with the measured garlic-citrus aromatic marinade and refrigerate tightly covered.
- **Orange handling** — Keep orange juice out of the 24-hour marinade; reserve it for the slow cook.
- **Pork fat** — Keep visible exterior and seam fat attached. The pork butt must self-render enough fat for crisping.
- **Equipment check** — Use a heavy Dutch oven that holds the pork in a snug single layer, a dry cast-iron or heavy skillet for charring, a food processor, a small saucepan, and a large heavy skillet for crisping.
- **Service ingredients** — Confirm corn tortillas, white onion, cilantro, limes, tomatoes, serranos, jalapeño, chipotle in adobo, Tajín, smoked paprika, chili powder, cumin, oregano, garlic powder, and onion powder before starting.

## Do Not Forget

- Keep the pork butt's visible fat attached; it is the source of the cooking and crisping fat.
- Do not add orange juice to the 24-hour marinade.
- Do not rinse or scrape the aromatic marinade from the pork before cooking.
- Use a snug pot and pour the liquid around the pork rather than over its seasoned surfaces.
- Tenderness is the deciding doneness test; do not stop at a number if the pork still resists a skewer.
- Keep the pork in substantial irregular pieces rather than finely shredding it.
- Separate rendered fat from pork juices before reducing the jus.
- Use only enough reduced jus to season and caramelize the crisped pork.
- Dry-char the salsa vegetables without oil and remove each item when ready; garlic finishes first.
- Cover the charred vegetables for 15 minutes so carryover steam softens them and loosens the skins.
- Keep useful char, discard only dry papery-black flakes, and pulse rather than continuously process.
- Tajín already contributes salt and acidity; rest and taste the salsa before adding more salt or cayenne.
- Crisp the pork and warm tortillas close to service.
- Taste the completed taco, not only the salsa by itself, before final adjustment.

## Full Cooking Timeline and Instructions

1. **Marinate the pork 24 hours ahead.** Combine **10 g kosher salt**, **6 finely grated garlic cloves**, **1 1/2 tsp orange marmalade**, **1 tsp lime zest**, **2 tsp crushed Mexican oregano**, **1 tsp black pepper**, **3/4 tsp ground cumin**, **1/8 tsp ground cinnamon**, and **1 Tbsp neutral oil**. Massage the paste over all surfaces of **2.25 lb pork butt cut into 2- to 3-inch cubes**. Cover tightly and refrigerate for **24 hours**. Do not add orange juice to this long marinade.

2. **Temper the pork and heat the oven.** Remove the marinated pork from the refrigerator **30 minutes** before cooking. Heat the oven to **300°F**. Do not rinse or scrape away the marinade.

3. **Stage the self-rendering cook.** Arrange the marinated pork snugly in a heavy Dutch oven, placing fattier surfaces against the bottom. Tuck **1/2 medium white onion in thick wedges**, **3 lightly crushed garlic cloves**, **1 bay leaf**, and **1/2 tsp Mexican oregano** around the pork.

4. **Add the citrus cooking liquid.** Combine **1/2 cup orange juice**, **3/4 cup water**, and **1 Tbsp fresh lime juice**. Pour the mixture around the pork instead of directly over the seasoned surfaces. Bring just to a gentle simmer on the stovetop.

5. **Cook covered.** Cover the Dutch oven and transfer it to the **300°F oven**. Cook for **1 hour 45 minutes** without aggressively boiling the pork.

6. **Uncover, render, and tenderize.** Remove the lid and gently turn the pieces. Continue cooking uncovered for **45 to 75 minutes**, turning approximately every **20 minutes**. If the pot becomes dry before the pork is tender, add **1/4 cup hot water**. Remove the pork when a skewer enters with almost no resistance and the meat separates under gentle pressure while still holding substantial pieces. The internal temperature will usually be around **195 to 205°F**, but tenderness is the deciding test.

7. **Dry-char the salsa vegetables.** Heat a dry cast-iron or other heavy skillet over medium-high heat. Add **2 Roma tomatoes**, **2 whole serranos**, **1 whole jalapeño**, **1/4 medium white onion cut-side down**, and **3 unpeeled garlic cloves**. Remove the garlic after approximately **3 to 5 minutes**, when spotted and softened. Rotate the chiles for **6 to 10 minutes**, the onion for **8 to 12 minutes**, and the tomatoes for **10 to 15 minutes**, removing each item when softened with patchy dark char.

8. **Steam-rest the charred vegetables.** Transfer the finished vegetables to a bowl as they become ready. Cover and rest for **15 minutes**. Peel the garlic, remove the chile stems, and retain the seeds for strong heat. Rub away only loose, dry, papery-black flakes; keep the flavorful blistered skins and all collected juices.

9. **Pulse the salsa base.** Place the charred vegetables and juices in a food processor with **1 chipotle in adobo**, **1 tsp adobo sauce**, **1 Tbsp apple-cider vinegar**, and **1/2 tsp kosher salt**. Pulse in short bursts until cohesive but still rustic. Add **1 Tbsp water** only if the processor needs help; use up to **2 Tbsp more** only if the salsa is too thick.

10. **Build the fiery chile-lime finish.** Add **1/2 packed cup cilantro**, **2 Tbsp lime juice**, **1 tsp Tajín**, **1 tsp chili powder**, **1/2 tsp smoked paprika**, **1/2 tsp ground cumin**, **1/2 tsp garlic powder**, **1/4 tsp onion powder**, **1/4 tsp crushed Mexican oregano**, **1/2 tsp sugar**, and a **small pinch black pepper**. Pulse only **3 to 5 short times** so the cilantro remains lively and the salsa stays textured.

11. **Rest and calibrate the salsa.** Let the salsa stand for **5 minutes**, then taste it on a small bite of carnitas or a tortilla. Add up to **1/4 tsp cayenne** only if it needs more heat. Add another pinch of salt only if it remains flat after the Tajín has hydrated. Add a small squeeze of lime if heavy, or another **1/4 tsp sugar** only if the char tastes harsh. Do not turn it sweet.

12. **Rest the pork and separate the pot liquid.** Transfer the tender pork to a tray and rest for **10 to 15 minutes**. Strain the Dutch-oven liquid into a heatproof container; discard the spent onion, garlic, and bay leaf. Let the liquid settle, then spoon the clear rendered pork fat into a separate bowl.

13. **Reduce the pork-citrus jus.** Pour the defatted pork juices into a small saucepan and simmer until reduced to approximately **1/4 cup**. Taste before seasoning. Add **1/2 to 1 tsp lime juice** only if it needs a fresher edge. Do not automatically add salt because the jus concentrates the pork's original seasoning.

14. **Prepare the final crisp.** Break the rested pork into irregular **1- to 1 1/2-inch pieces**, preserving natural edges and fatty seams. Do not finely shred it. Heat a large skillet to approximately **300°F** and test one piece before loading the pan.

15. **Hard-crisp the carnitas.** Add **2 Tbsp reserved rendered pork fat** to the skillet. Arrange the pork in one uncrowded layer and cook undisturbed for **3 to 4 minutes**, until the first surface develops a dark substantial crust. Turn and crisp another side for **2 to 3 minutes**.

16. **Caramelize with controlled jus.** Drizzle **2 Tbsp reduced pork-citrus jus around the pork**, not over every crisp surface. Toss once and cook **30 to 60 seconds**, until the jus evaporates and caramelizes. Taste one piece. Add no more than **1 additional Tbsp jus** if the meat needs greater intensity.

17. **Warm the tortillas.** Warm **10 to 12 fresh white-corn tortillas** in the skillet for **20 to 30 seconds per side** until pliable with scattered toasted spots. If rendered pork fat remains after crisping, lightly brush it over the tortillas before warming. Hold beneath a clean towel.

18. **Build and serve.** Fill each warm tortilla with a generous portion of crisp carnitas. Add fiery charred chile-lime salsa, finely diced white onion, chopped cilantro, and a squeeze of fresh lime. Serve immediately with extra salsa and lime at the table.

## Final Taste Target

The pork should taste unmistakably like pork first, followed by roasted garlic, clear orange-lime lift, Mexican oregano, recognizable cumin, and a quiet cinnamon undertone.

Each taco should move from succulent interior pork to dark crisp edges, concentrated savory citrus jus, then a fiery salsa with roasted tomato and fresh-chile depth, restrained chipotle smoke, bright cilantro, chile-lime tang, smoked paprika, warm cumin-oregano seasoning, gentle sweetness, raw onion crunch, and clean lime.

The salsa should evoke the vivid red-chile-and-lime punch Amy remembered from fiery fast-food tacos, but it must still taste like a freshly charred Amy & Don Kitchen salsa rather than bottled taco sauce.

The dish should not taste like sweet pulled pork, wet braised pork, generic taco seasoning, canned fruit, bland roasted vegetables, or a chipotle-dominated adobo sauce.

## Chef's Notes

The water and citrus liquid protect the pork during the first phase while its own fat renders. Once uncovered, the remaining liquid reduces and the pork begins behaving more like small-batch carnitas cooked in freshly rendered lard.

The measured orange marmalade contributes concentrated peel aroma. Keeping it restrained prevents sweetness from overtaking the pork or interfering with clean crisping.

The reduced pork-citrus jus is a seasoning tool, not a finishing sauce. Add it around already-crisp pork so it can evaporate and caramelize without soaking the crust.

The salsa was the decisive live-test refinement. The original charred serrano-chipotle base tasted too generic until cilantro, sugar, lime, Tajín, smoked paprika, chili powder, cumin, oregano, garlic powder, onion powder, and black pepper gave it a specific fiery chile-lime identity.

For leftovers, store tender pork, rendered fat, jus, and salsa separately when practical. Crisp only the portion being served.

## Source / Research Notes

- Traditional carnitas technique protects pork identity through slow fat-assisted cooking and a vigorous final crisp.
- The small-batch self-rendering method uses water for early protection, then relies on the pork butt's own rendered fat after evaporation.
- Chef-level versions reviewed emphasized substantial pork pieces, salt penetration, rendered-fat cooking, concentrated pork flavor, and final crisping near service.
- Rick Bayless carnitas and crisped-meat techniques informed the pork-forward restraint and late hard crisp.
- Jonathan Zaragoza's carnitas work informed the overnight seasoning and slow lard-based texture target.
- The Amy & Don house variation deliberately restores Don's requested garlic, citrus, cumin, and cinnamon identity in controlled layers.
- The locked salsa preserves the charred serrano-jalapeño-chipotle base but adds the cilantro, sugar, spice, smoked-paprika, and Tajín architecture discovered during the first live cook.
- Orange marmalade and orange juice replace fresh orange zest and fruit for this version; canned mandarins remain intentionally excluded.
